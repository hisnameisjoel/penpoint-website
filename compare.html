Worldbuilding and story organization

Scrivener doesn't do worldbuilding, and Penpoint is built around it. There's an outliner and index cards, but nothing built for tracking characters or story arcs, where Penpoint gives you real character, location, and item entries as part of the $40. Their mind-mapping app Scapple is ~$21 extra and connects to Scrivener in only a basic way. Our full Penpoint vs Scrivener comparison covers what Scrivener still does better and how to move a project across.

Penpoint and Dabble both give you a place to keep your cast, but Dabble's starts on the $19/month Writer plan and stops sooner. You get character profiles, a free-form Notebook, a Plot Grid, and @mentions that follow a rename. What there isn't is a relationship map, a timeline, a calendar, or item tracking, and locations and items are notes you write rather than entries the app understands. All five of those are in Penpoint, and they are entries the app reads. There's more in the full Penpoint vs Dabble comparison, including real-time co-authoring and what happens when a subscription lapses.

Campfire comes closest to Penpoint on worldbuilding. They have structured character profiles, timelines, a relationship web, and modules for species, magic systems, and languages. Each module is a separate purchase, though there's a free tier that lets you try all of them with limits. Where they part company is the writing side: Campfire's manuscript editor is basic, locations can't be placed inside other locations, and there's no chapter scanning or character name suggestions as you type. See the full Penpoint vs Campfire comparison for which modules you actually need and what they add up to.

World Anvil is a wiki-style tool built for tabletop RPG worldbuilding, with 28 worldbuilding templates and locations inside other locations; Penpoint is built for novelists and puts the manuscript beside the world rather than behind it. World Anvil's manuscript editor is basic, and you can only export as a web file, where Penpoint exports Word, PDF, EPUB, and Markdown. Paid plans do include co-author slots, but real-time collaboration and manuscript tools are thin. Our full Penpoint vs World Anvil comparison covers what their templates do better and where the manuscript side stops.

Ellipsus doesn't aim at worldbuilding at all, and Penpoint is mostly worldbuilding. Documents, drafts, and folders are the entire hierarchy, so characters, locations, timelines, and invented calendars end up as more documents that nothing in the app can read back to you. Penpoint makes each of those its own entry instead, linked to the chapters it appears in. Their public roadmap files Charts and Maps under Unlikely. For the rest, including their real-time co-authoring and what it takes to get a whole library out, read the full Penpoint vs Ellipsus comparison.

Penpoint comes with relationship maps, story & character arc visualizers, dynamic @ name linking, chapter timelines, item tracking, locations inside other locations, customizable per-chapter layouts with per-scene character, location, item, and POV tracking, a fully featured note editor with image and folder storage, and the Minipen, a miniature version of your worldbuilding that goes alongside the Manuscript editor. And when one book becomes many, convert it into a Series: one shared cast and world across every installment, each book keeping its own manuscript, chapters, and arcs, plus Series Arcs for the threads that span the whole saga. All of it, $40.

Writing a whole series

Most writing tools leave a series for you to organize. In Scrivener you build it by hand: a folder per book, with every character re-created or copied between files. World Anvil spreads it across multiple worlds, which is not series-specific, so your cast gets rebuilt for each installment. Ellipsus has no real chapters, let alone books, so a series there is folders of documents and a running order nothing in the app tracks. Campfire and Dabble are the exceptions: Campfire's Linked Projects share elements between separate book projects, and one Dabble project holds several books with characters, notebook, and plot grids shared across all of them.

Penpoint does it automatically. Turn what you've already written into Book 1 without losing a word, then keep going: a trilogy, a saga, a seven-book world, all in one project with a single shared cast and map. Write a character once and they stay current across every book, no matter which one you're editing. A built-in book switcher moves you between installments in a click, and a This Book / All Books toggle reads each character's Presence to either narrow the whole app down to just the book you're drafting or show the entire series cast at once.

We don't know of another writing app built to worldbuild across a series this way. And it's fully reversible: split a series back into standalone books whenever you want.

Writing and manuscript tools

Penpoint imports manuscripts from Word, EPUB, Markdown, and plain text with automatic chapter detection. Campfire matches this on Word and EPUB. Scrivener imports Word and several other formats, splitting into chapters automatically or at break points you define. Dabble imports Word, RTF, Markdown, and HTML up to 50 MB, splitting on headings as it goes. Ellipsus takes documents in and files them into folders, but doesn't publish the list of formats it accepts. World Anvil has no file import at all; you're copy-pasting.

Penpoint also has a Chapter Scanner that none of the other five offer: scan a chapter and it finds the characters, locations, and items in your prose and proposes them as worldbuilding entries, sorted by how confident it is. You review the list and apply the ones you want, and each one you keep is linked to the chapter it appeared in. Import and get straight to work.

For export: Penpoint, Scrivener, and Campfire all handle Word, PDF, and more. Dabble sends a book to Word, Google Docs, Markdown, or a shareable web page, and has no PDF button of its own; their docs point you at Print, then Save as PDF. World Anvil exports as a web file that you'd need to convert before sending anything to an agent. Ellipsus gives you .docx, PDF, or Markdown one document at a time, plus a direct Export to AO3 button for fan writers; its help center says bulk downloads aren't possible, so a trilogy spread across forty documents is forty separate exports.

Penpoint includes a fully offline spellchecker with a custom dictionary you control, on Windows, macOS, and Linux. English is built into the app and the other 59 languages download once, after which they work with no connection. Every character, location, and item name in your book is automatically recognized, so invented names and fantasy words stop showing up as errors the minute you create them. On Windows and macOS, third-party writing tools like ProWritingAid and Grammarly can also work alongside Penpoint via their desktop apps. Scrivener also has a real offline spell-checker, but no Linux version. World Anvil has none of its own and leans on your OS or browser; Campfire documents none anywhere. Ellipsus does have one, covering 18 languages, but it's off until you switch it on and each language downloads into the document you're in rather than into your account. Dabble's checking is powered by ProWritingAid and runs inside Dabble: spell check is free on every plan, while grammar and style are a paid feature.

One thing Penpoint does not do is let you write with someone else. Dabble rebuilt around collaboration in 2026 and now has real-time co-authoring, track changes, and separate roles for editors, reviewers, and beta readers. Campfire lets you invite collaborators to view, comment on, or edit. Ellipsus goes furthest of the three: real-time co-writing, five permission levels from Owner down to Viewer, and an unlimited number of collaborators without paying anything. Penpoint is a single-writer desktop app, and its inline comments are notes to yourself rather than a conversation. If your book is a two-person job, that is a real reason to pick one of them over us.

Privacy, ownership, and offline access

Penpoint is a desktop app. Your books are saved as files on your hard drive; open them, back them up, or move them like any other file. No account needed, and the app is a one-time purchase rather than a subscription. Your writing stays on your device unless you turn on Penpoint Cloud, the optional paid backup and sync service. AI never touches your writing, and if you turn Penpoint Cloud on, your writing is end-to-end encrypted, so we cannot read it.

Scrivener is also fully local and offline. Dabble rebuilt this in 2026: it now saves every change to your device before the server sees it, then catches up when you reconnect (their docs). The difference that remains is where the book lives, because your account and your projects are still on their servers. Campfire has desktop and mobile apps, but your writing lives on their servers. World Anvil is web-only with no desktop app and no offline mode. If their servers go down, your worldbuilding is unavailable until they come back. Ellipsus is browser-only too, and its help center is explicit that the app needs a live connection to reach its services. They are working on it: in September 2026 their public roadmap marked offline support, a desktop app, and a native mobile app all in progress. Penpoint is desktop-only today: a mobile companion app entered beta testing in July 2026, but it is not on the public app stores and it needs a Penpoint Cloud subscription, so it is not covered by the one-time desktop license.

Penpoint's $40 covers Windows, macOS, and Linux. Scrivener charges per OS. The cloud tools all keep your work on their servers. Dabble, Campfire, and World Anvil each sell a lifetime option; Ellipsus charges nothing at all unless you want Plus, which is $99 once and tied to the account that bought it. What a lapsed subscription costs you varies, so check before you rely on it: Dabble's pricing page states you keep read and export access forever and lose only the ability to write new words.

Pricing and value

Penpoint costs $40, once. Full app, all platforms. Updates are free for the major version you purchase.

Scrivener runs $59.99 per platform with no worldbuilding. Dabble's lifetime plan is $699, cloud-based, with basic worldbuilding. Campfire's full module set runs $420 but comes with a basic manuscript editor and no chapter scanner. World Anvil's Grandmaster lifetime is $650 (web-only, web-file export only). Their top Sage tier is around $1,350. Ellipsus undercuts everyone including us: the free plan has unlimited documents and 30 days of version history, and Plus is $99 once. What the money isn't buying there is a place to keep your world or a copy of your book on your own machine.
